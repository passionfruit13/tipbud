How Tip Outs Work: Who Tips Out Whom, and How Much

Updated July 2026 · Written for U.S. tipped workers, not managers

Most tip-out guides are written for the people who set the policy. This one is for the person handing over the cash. What a tip out is, the two ways houses calculate it, the typical ranges by role, and how to tell whether yours is out of line.

What a tip out is (and how it's different from tip pooling)

A tip out is money you pass from your own tips to the people who supported your tables — busser, bartender, food runner, host. You earned the tips; you share a cut, usually set by a house policy, sometimes at your discretion.

Tip pooling is different. In a pool, everyone's tips go into one pot first, and the house splits the pot by hours or points. You never really "own" your tips; you own a share of the pool. Kickfin's overview of pooling vs sharing vs tipping out walks through the variations.

Why the distinction matters to you: with a tip out, your take home moves with your own tables. With a pool, it moves with the whole floor. If you're deciding between two jobs, ask which system the house runs before you ask about the menu.

% of tips vs % of sales — the two methods

Houses calculate tip outs one of two ways. Same idea, very different math on a bad night.

Method 1: a percentage of your tips

You tip out a share of what you actually made. Say you took in $200 in tips and the house asks for 20% to support staff: you pass along $40 and keep $160. Light night, light tip out. This method scales with reality.

Method 2: a percentage of your sales

You tip out a share of what you sold, regardless of what you were tipped. Say you rang $1,000 in sales and the house takes 3% of sales: you owe $30 whether your tables tipped 25% or walked on the tip. On a $200-tip night that's fine. On an $80-tip night, that same $30 is a much bigger bite out of what you take home.

Both methods are common and both are normal — 7shifts and Toast describe each in their guides. The thing to know is simply which one your house uses, because it changes what a slow night costs you.

Typical tip-out percentages by role

There is no law or industry body that sets these numbers. What exists is a cluster of published guides, mostly from scheduling and POS companies, that describe what houses commonly do. Treat everything below as a range, not a rule — every house differs, and fine dining, dives, and high-volume bars all sit at different points.

  • Bussers: roughly 1–2% of your sales, or 10–20% of your tips, per 7shifts' tip-out guide and Homebase's guide to tipping out.
  • Bartenders: commonly quoted around 7–10% of your alcohol or beverage sales — some houses use a flat cut of tips instead. See Homebase and Toast's tip-out explainer.
  • Hosts and food runners: roughly 1–2% of sales each, when they're in the tip out at all, per 7shifts and Homebase.

If your house asks for numbers inside these ranges, you're in ordinary territory. If a number looks high, the next section is the sanity check.

Is my tip out too high?

Look at the total, not the line items. Add up everything you pass along in a night and compare it to your tips. Kickfin's guide puts common overall tip outs around 20–30% of a server's tips; Restaurant Business's Advice Guy lands in the same neighborhood. Fine dining, with more support roles per table, often runs higher. Again: ranges, not rules.

Two situations deserve a closer look:

  • Sales-based tip outs on bad-tip nights. A 3%-of-sales tip out is 15% of your tips on a 20%-tip night, but 37% of your tips on a night where tables averaged 8%. If large parties, walkouts, or a slow section regularly push your effective rate past a third of your tips, the policy is costing you real money — worth raising.
  • You can't actually say what you tip out. If you don't track it, you can't compare it. Log a few weeks of shifts — tips in, tip-outs out — and the pattern is right there. Our guide to keeping track of your tips covers what to write down.

The percentage only means something against your actual nights. Run your own numbers through the tip-out calculator to see what a given policy does to a real shift.

What U.S. federal law says

Two rules matter, and both are federal — states can add their own layers, and this page isn't legal or tax advice.

Managers and owners can't take tips. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, managers, supervisors, and owners may not keep any portion of employees' tips — not through a tip pool, not through a tip out, not directly. The U.S. Department of Labor's FLSA tip rules spell this out, and its Wage and Hour Division takes complaints. A manager who covers a section as a server for a shift is a narrow exception for tips they earned on their own tables; skimming the pool is not.

You're taxed on what you kept, not what passed through. Under U.S. federal rules, IRS Publication 531 has you keep a daily tip record that includes what you paid out to other workers, and you report your tips net of tip-outs. Tip out $40 of a $200 night and $160 is your reportable number. That's also why the tip-out column in your records matters at tax time — the U.S. federal tip deduction only works from tips you reported. Not tax advice — confirm the details with your tax preparer.

Common questions

What is a standard tip out percentage?

Most published guides put a server's total tip-out between 20% and 30% of tips, built from smaller cuts per role: 1–2% of sales to bussers, 7–10% of alcohol sales to bartenders, and 1–2% to runners and hosts. There is no legal standard — every house sets its own.

Typical tip-out ranges by role, per published industry guides
RoleTypical tip-out range
Busser1–2% of sales, or 10–20% of tips
Bartender / service bar7–10% of alcohol or beverage sales
Food runner1–2% of sales
Host1–2% of sales, when tipped out at all
All roles combinedroughly 20–30% of a server's tips

How much should I tip out the busser?

Industry guides put it at roughly 1–2% of your sales, or 10–20% of your tips, depending on the method your house uses (7shifts, Homebase). There's no legal standard — every house differs, and the number that matters is the one in your house policy.

Is tipping out a percentage of my sales normal?

Yes — sales-based tip outs are one of the two common methods, alongside tips-based. The trade-off: you owe the same amount whether tables tipped well or stiffed you, so a bad-tip night costs you more of what you actually took home.

Is tipping out a percentage of sales legal?

Yes — U.S. federal law does not bar tip-outs figured as a percentage of sales; the Fair Labor Standards Act regulates who may receive tips, not the formula. Managers, supervisors, and owners can never take a share. Some states add their own limits, so check state rules. This is general information, not legal advice.

Can my manager take part of the tip out?

No. Under the U.S. federal FLSA, managers, supervisors, and owners can't keep any portion of employees' tips — pool, tip out, or otherwise (DOL). If it's happening, the Wage and Hour Division takes complaints.

Do I pay taxes on tips I tipped out?

Under U.S. federal rules, no. IRS Publication 531 has you record what you paid out and report your tips net of tip-outs — you're taxed on what you kept. Not tax advice; confirm with your preparer.
